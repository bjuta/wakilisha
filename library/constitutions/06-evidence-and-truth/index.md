# Evidence and Truth

## Say what we know, and say what we do not know

Trust is the whole game.

WAKILISHA can have beautiful pages, sharp writing, strong charts, good search traffic, and active community, but if people cannot trust the work, the institution weakens at the root.

Trust begins with a simple discipline.

Say what we know.

Say how we know it.

Say what we do not know.

Do not make thin knowledge sound full.

Do not make uncertain knowledge sound final.

Do not hide missing evidence behind confident prose.

This is especially important for African cultural memory because many sources are incomplete, scattered, platform-dependent, orally held, poorly credited, or distorted by markets that reward what travels but forget what made travel possible.

WAKILISHA should not add to that distortion.

## Types of evidence

Not all evidence is the same.

A platform listing is evidence, but it may be incomplete.

A chart entry is evidence, but it reflects a method.

An artist claim is evidence, but it may need context.

An interview is evidence, but memory can shift.

A credit is evidence, but credits can be wrong or missing.

A fan memory is evidence of experience, not always evidence of fact.

A pattern in data is evidence for a question, not proof by itself.

An AI summary is not evidence. It is a generated interpretation of material that must be checked.

WAKILISHA should build a culture where these differences are normal to name.

## Unknown is better than invented

Unknown release date is better than invented release date.

Unverified credit is better than false authority.

A thin page is better than a padded page.

A correction is better than a quiet lie.

A note that says we are still checking is better than a confident paragraph built on nothing.

This sounds obvious, but product pressure often pushes the other way. Empty fields look ugly. Thin pages feel unfinished. Search previews need copy. Users expect answers. AI can fill gaps in seconds.

The temptation to invent is real because invention often looks better than honesty.

WAKILISHA must choose honesty.

## Thin data

Thin data is not failure.

Thin data is a state of knowledge.

A page with thin data should tell the truth and invite future improvement. It should not pretend to be complete. It should not bury the uncertainty. It should not pad the page with generic prose that could fit any artist, track, or release.

A thin artist page can say what is known, show available relationships, and name what is missing.

A thin release page can show a tracklist if verified, mark unknown dates, and avoid speculation about impact.

A thin chart note can state the observed movement without inventing a cultural explanation.

The goal is not to make every page feel equally full. The goal is to make every page equally honest.

## Corrections

Corrections should not be treated as embarrassment.

They are part of institutional honesty.

WAKILISHA should create a clear corrections habit. When a public claim changes, the change should be visible where it matters. If a credit is corrected, a date is revised, or a relationship is removed, the institution should preserve enough memory for future people to understand what changed and why.

Quiet correction may be acceptable for small typo-level fixes. Factual corrections that affect understanding should be visible.

The archive should not pretend it was always right.

## Method

Charts need method.

Editorial arguments need method.

AI summaries need method.

Question trails need method.

If WAKILISHA shows a ranking, it should explain what the ranking uses, what it excludes, and what a reader should not overclaim from it.

If WAKILISHA publishes an interpretation, it should show enough of the reasoning for a thoughtful reader to understand how the conclusion was reached.

If WAKILISHA uses AI to draft or summarize, the system should preserve the evidence boundary and the review boundary.

Method does not have to be heavy. But it has to exist.

## Product implications

The product should make evidence visible.

A page should distinguish confirmed data from inferred context.

A chart should distinguish movement from interpretation.

A credit should distinguish verified from unverified when the distinction matters.

A source badge should not be decorative. It should tell the reader where a claim or playback source comes from.

An AI-generated summary should not appear identical to human-reviewed editorial judgment unless it has passed review.

A page with missing evidence should not hide the gap.

Evidence should become part of the experience architecture, not a footnote nobody sees.

## Editorial implications

Editors should write with claim discipline.

Before a sentence ships, ask what kind of sentence it is.

Is it a fact?

Is it interpretation?

Is it memory?

Is it argument?

Is it inference?

Is it speculation?

Each type has a different burden.

A factual sentence needs support.

An interpretive sentence needs reasoning.

A memory needs attribution or framing.

A speculative sentence needs to be named as speculation, and many speculative sentences do not belong at all.

The reader should never have to guess whether WAKILISHA knows something or is only suggesting it.

## Community implications

Community can improve evidence.

People can bring corrections, memories, sources, photos, timelines, translations, and local context.

But community contribution needs care. A memory is valuable, but memory is not always verification. A confident comment is not always true. A local claim can be important and still need checking.

WAKILISHA should welcome contribution without surrendering judgment.

The right posture is gratitude plus verification.

## AI implications

AI is most dangerous when evidence is thin and prose needs filling.

The model can make the empty space feel full. That is exactly when WAKILISHA must be most careful.

AI may help identify missing fields. It may summarize known data. It may propose questions. It may draft cautious language. It may compare possible relationships.

AI must not invent cultural memory.

AI must not create false credits.

AI must not turn platform metadata into biography.

AI must not make every artist sound important in the same vague way.

AI must not hide uncertainty because uncertainty looks less polished.

If the evidence is thin, the AI output should be thin and honest too.

## Decision tests

What is the claim?

What supports it?

What kind of evidence is being used?

What is missing?

Have we named uncertainty clearly?

Would a person represented here recognize the claim as fair?

Can a future editor understand why we wrote this?

If challenged, could WAKILISHA explain the basis of the claim?

## Failure modes

WAKILISHA is drifting when every page sounds equally complete.

It is drifting when missing data is hidden.

It is drifting when AI-generated language fills gaps without evidence.

It is drifting when chart rankings are treated as total truth.

It is drifting when corrections are quiet because correction feels inconvenient.

It is drifting when public confidence grows faster than evidence.

## Closing note

Truth in cultural work is not only about avoiding false facts.

It is about carrying uncertainty with respect.

WAKILISHA should be trusted not because it knows everything, but because it does not pretend to know what it has not earned.
